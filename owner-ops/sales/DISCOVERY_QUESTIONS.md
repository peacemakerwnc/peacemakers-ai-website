# Discovery questions — next residential builder

Internal. For James Fullen on the next Blueprint conversation with a residential builder.

Open by asking why they took the meeting. Restate the problem in their words until they agree it is theirs. Ask what they have already tried. Describe the business result before you name a tool. Leave price, and the yes, for a separate step you decide.

Then ask these five. Stop when the answer is specific enough to use.

## 1. Where was the choice made, and where did someone type it again?

Take the last house you finished selections on. Where was the choice actually made, and where did somebody type it again?

**Why you're asking.** The repeating cost is often the same facts rebuilt for the designer, the client, the reviewer, and the job file. You need the system of record and the handmade copies named separately before you recommend anything.

**A good answer sounds like.** "The client approves in our job system. The designer keeps a spreadsheet so the meeting is readable. Our coordinator retypes each line, including the pictures, so the client can sign. The spreadsheet is the working copy. The job system is the record."

## 2. What did you already try, and why did it stop?

What have you already bought, piloted, or been pitched for this, and why isn't it running?

**Why you're asking.** A stalled purchase-order sheet, an unfinished file share, or an accounting add-on that was too large for one step shows you which path to leave alone. People stay on the path that already matches an invoice when the new path never reaches the books.

**A good answer sounds like.** "We talked to a vendor about field purchase orders and never got past the first call. We also looked at an accounting add-on and left it, because we would have been paying for project management we already run somewhere else. This week the supers still write material orders on paper, and the office still matches the invoice by hand."

## 3. Whose name has to be on it before it counts?

Before a client, an outside reviewer, or your books can treat this as final, whose name has to be on it?

**Why you're asking.** A clean packet can sit in front of the approval system. It cannot replace the signature, and it cannot post to the accounting system. If you skip the person who uploads, the person who approves, or the person who keys job cost, the first build has to be undone later.

**A good answer sounds like.** "Our coordinator checks the packet. The client approves in the job system. Our accountant keys the approved change into the accounting system, and she is the only one who can. I want a person on each of those steps."

## 4. Which one packet would you put in a designer's hands first?

If we only made one of these packets consistent, which one would you want in a designer's hands first, and what would I see on the next job that told us it worked?

**Why you're asking.** One worksheet, done on a real job, is the win. The look can carry to the next category after that pattern holds. Purchase orders, extra trades, and a reporting dashboard are later conversations. This question gets the sequence and the done-test in the same breath.

**A good answer sounds like.** "Tile first, then the architectural review form we actually submit. I'll know it worked when the designer fills one sheet, the client gets the same PDF every time, and our coordinator uploads it without retyping. Paint and electrical wait until that holds. Purchase orders wait until after that."

## 5. What number will you use, and can you pull it?

What one number will you use to decide this was worth it, and can you get that number from last month?

**Why you're asking.** Leadership needs one gauge they will actually look at. A missing count stays missing. Leave the baseline blank until they produce it.

**A good answer sounds like.** "I don't have a counted baseline. I can time the next tile package, or I can pull last month's invoices and tell you how many failed the first match. I'll judge the first step by whether the worksheets are in use."

## When the ask is outside the Blueprint

Say this, and stop:

"That sits outside this Blueprint, so I'll note it for a later decision, and this engagement will stay on the work you select from the Blueprint."
